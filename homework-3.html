Homework 03

Random walks and limit theorems

Simulating penetration scores to explore the Law of Large Numbers and the Central Limit Theorem.

1. Models and interpretation

Each of m attackers attempts to penetrate n systems in sequence. One random increment is generated for each attacker and system. Increments are independent across systems and attackers, with the same distribution throughout each experiment.

In the Bernoulli model, a success adds 1 and a failure adds 0. In the symmetric Rademacher model, each step adds either +1 or −1 with equal probability. The second model describes a net score, rather than a count of penetrated systems.

This is a simplified stochastic model. Shared vulnerabilities, attacker adaptation and dependence between attempts are not included.

2. Run the simulation

Each run generates a new random sample. The limits on n and m keep the interactive experiment manageable in the browser.

The empirical probability is the fraction of attackers for whom |Sₖ/k − μ| > ε. Chebyshev's inequality gives the theoretical upper bound min(1, σ²/(kε²)) for this probability. The bound is not a prediction of the exact probability.

Rows share the same trajectories and are therefore dependent. Finite-sample estimates can fluctuate and need not decrease at every checkpoint. An observed rate of 0% does not establish that the true probability is zero.

4. What to look for

If an increment has mean μ and variance σ², the total Sₙ has mean nμ and variance nσ². The average Sₙ/n has mean μ and variance σ²/n. The normalized total Sₙ/√n has mean √n μ and variance σ².

Increasing n reduces the variance of the average. Increasing m provides more observations of the final-score distribution, improving the precision of its empirical estimates. It does not change the theoretical distribution of one attacker's score.

For Rademacher increments, μ = 0 and σ² = 1. The Central Limit Theorem therefore applies directly to Sₙ/√n. For Bernoulli increments, μ = p and σ² = p(1 − p). When 0 < p < 1, the standardized quantity is (Sₙ − np)/√(np(1 − p)).

5. Recorded results and conclusions

The following observations refer to two recorded runs with n = 1000 systems, m = 2000 attackers and tolerance ε = 0.1. The Bernoulli run used p = 0.3. These recorded values remain fixed in this discussion; the interactive results above change when a new simulation is run.

Total scores become more dispersed

In the Rademacher experiment, the empirical variance of the total increased from 10.3256 at 10 systems to 1022.9588 at 1000 systems. The corresponding theoretical values are 10 and 1000.

In the Bernoulli experiment, the empirical variance increased from 2.0500 to 216.0718, compared with theoretical values of 2.1 and 210. Both experiments support the relationship Var(Sₙ) = nσ²: total scores become more dispersed as the number of systems increases.

Averages concentrate: the Law of Large Numbers

Dividing by n reduces the variance to σ²/n. In the Rademacher experiment, the fraction of attackers with |Sₙ/n| > 0.1 decreased from 74.95% at 10 systems to 0.20% at 1000 systems: only 4 out of 2000 attackers remained outside the selected tolerance.

In the Bernoulli experiment, the fraction with |Sₙ/n − 0.3| > 0.1 decreased from 29.00% at 10 systems to 0.00% observed at both 500 and 1000 systems. No exceedances were observed in those samples, but this does not establish that the underlying probability is zero.

These results illustrate convergence in probability: for a fixed positive tolerance, the probability of a deviation beyond that tolerance tends to zero as n increases. A finite simulation illustrates this behavior; it does not constitute a proof of the limiting theorem.

A mean close to the target is not enough

At 10 systems, the Rademacher empirical mean of the individual averages was already −0.0080, close to the theoretical target 0. Nevertheless, 74.95% of attackers were outside the tolerance. Positive and negative deviations can cancel when averaged across attackers.

Therefore, concentration must be assessed using dispersion and deviation probabilities, rather than only the overall mean. Increasing n reduces the variability of each attacker's average; increasing m improves the estimation of its distribution.

Square-root normalization preserves variance

For Rademacher increments, the empirical variance of Sₙ/√n remained close to 1 at every checkpoint. At n = 1000, its empirical mean was 0.0126 and its variance was 1.0230, compared with theoretical values 0 and 1.

The Central Limit Theorem states that Sₙ/√n converges in distribution to the standard normal distribution in this model. The histogram and the orange normal curve provide a visual comparison. Agreement of the mean and variance alone would not establish that a distribution is normal.

This convergence concerns the distribution across repeated trajectories. It does not mean that each normalized trajectory converges to zero.

Why Bernoulli requires centering

For Bernoulli increments with p = 0.3, the variance of Sₙ/√n remains 0.21, but its mean is 0.3√n. At n = 1000, the observed mean was 9.4778, close to the theoretical value 9.4868. The observed variance was 0.2161, close to 0.21.

Thus, constant variance does not imply convergence to a standard normal distribution: the center of this distribution moves to the right as n increases. For fixed 0 < p < 1, the appropriate standardized quantity is Zₙ = (Sₙ − np)/√(np(1 − p)). The Central Limit Theorem applies to Zₙ.

Chebyshev bounds and observed probabilities

At 1000 systems, the Rademacher empirical exceedance rate was 0.20%, while the Chebyshev upper bound was 10%. For Bernoulli, the observed rate was 0%, while the bound was 2.10%.

Chebyshev's inequality provides an upper bound on the true deviation probability, not its exact value. The bound can be much larger than the observed rate. It also does not impose an absolute limit on a random finite-sample frequency.

Interpretation: systems and attackers

In the Bernoulli model, increasing n increases the expected number of successful penetrations per attacker, np. At the same time, the proportion of successful attempts becomes more predictable and concentrates around p. A stable success proportion therefore does not imply a small total number of penetrations.

Across all m independent attackers, the aggregate Bernoulli score has mean mnp and variance mnp(1 − p). With the recorded parameters, these are 600000 and 420000. The aggregate counts successful attacker-system attempts: it does not count distinct compromised systems, since several attackers may succeed against the same system.

In the Rademacher model, the aggregate score has mean 0 and variance mn, equal to 2000000 here. Positive and negative scores can cancel. A small net score should therefore not be interpreted as evidence that few successful events occurred.

Limits of the experiment

The model assumes independent increments with an unchanged distribution, and independence between attackers. Real attacks may involve shared vulnerabilities, different attacker capabilities, learning or defensive changes. These effects are not represented.

Empirical estimates fluctuate between runs, and checkpoints share the same trajectories. Histogram appearance also depends on the number of attackers, the chosen intervals and the automatic axis scales. The observations support the theoretical predictions within this simplified model.

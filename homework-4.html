Online statistics and sampling distributions

Updating mean and variance with Welford's algorithm, then studying their behavior across repeated samples.

Part 1 · Welford's recurrence formulas

An online algorithm updates its statistics whenever a new observation arrives. Here, “online” means processing data sequentially; it does not refer to an internet connection.

Welford's algorithm maintains the observation count, the current mean and the sum of squared deviations from the current mean. Previous observations do not need to be stored by the algorithm.

SSₖ = Σᵢ₌₁ᵏ (xᵢ − x̄ₖ)²

SS is the sum of squared deviations from the mean, not the uncentered sum Σxᵢ².

1. Updating the mean

Let xₖ be the new observation and x̄ₖ₋₁ the mean of the previous k − 1 observations. For k ≥ 2, the updated mean is:

x̄ₖ = [(k − 1)x̄ₖ₋₁ + xₖ] / k
x̄ₖ = x̄ₖ₋₁ + (xₖ − x̄ₖ₋₁) / k

Define δₖ = xₖ − x̄ₖ₋₁, the difference between the new observation and the previous mean. Then:

x̄ₖ = x̄ₖ₋₁ + δₖ/k

The new observation moves the mean by a fraction of its distance from the old mean. As k increases, each new observation has a smaller weight.

2. Updating the sum of squared deviations

Separate the new observation from the previous ones:

SSₖ = Σᵢ₌₁ᵏ⁻¹ (xᵢ − x̄ₖ)² + (xₖ − x̄ₖ)²

Since x̄ₖ = x̄ₖ₋₁ + δₖ/k, every previous deviation changes by −δₖ/k. Expanding the squares gives:

SSₖ = SSₖ₋₁ − (2δₖ/k) Σᵢ₌₁ᵏ⁻¹ (xᵢ − x̄ₖ₋₁) + (k − 1)δₖ²/k² + (xₖ − x̄ₖ)²

The sum of deviations from the old mean is zero, so the cross term vanishes. Also, xₖ − x̄ₖ = (k − 1)δₖ/k. Therefore:

SSₖ = SSₖ₋₁ + (k − 1)δₖ²/k² + (k − 1)²δₖ²/k²
SSₖ = SSₖ₋₁ + [(k − 1)/k]δₖ²
SSₖ = SSₖ₋₁ + δₖ(xₖ − x̄ₖ)

The first difference uses the old mean. The second difference uses the updated mean.

3. From SS to variance

The descriptive variance divides SS by k. The corrected sample variance divides SS by k − 1. Under independent, identically distributed sampling, the corrected version is an unbiased estimator of the population variance.

vₖ = SSₖ/k, for k ≥ 1
sₖ² = SSₖ/(k − 1), for k ≥ 2

Substituting the recurrence for SS gives the corresponding variance recurrences:

vₖ = [(k − 1)/k]vₖ₋₁ + δₖ(xₖ − x̄ₖ)/k, for k ≥ 2

sₖ² = [(k − 2)/(k − 1)]sₖ₋₁² + δₖ(xₖ − x̄ₖ)/(k − 1), for k ≥ 3

Initially, the first observation gives x̄₁ = x₁ and SS₁ = 0. The corrected sample variance is undefined with only one observation. For two observations, s₂² = SS₂.

4. The three-line update

Start with mean = 0 and SS = 0. For each observation x, let k be the number of observations including the new one. The core update is: 

const delta = x - mean;
mean += delta / k;
SS += delta * (x - mean);

After the update, the corrected sample variance is SS/(k − 1), provided k ≥ 2. The initial value mean = 0 is a computational convention: an empty sample has no statistical mean.

The algorithm requires constant memory for its running state. It also avoids the potentially unstable subtraction of large, nearly equal quantities in Σxᵢ² − kx̄ₖ².

Part 2 · Sampling mean and variance

Each of m attackers receives one score for each of n systems. Scores are independent and uniformly distributed over the integers {0, 1, …, 10}. The same distribution is used for every attacker and system.

P(X = j) = 1/11, for j = 0, …, 10
μ = E[X] = 5
E[X²] = 35
σ² = E[X²] − μ² = 10

For each attacker, Welford's recurrence computes the sample mean and the corrected sample variance SS/(n − 1). Across attackers, we then study the averages and dispersion of these two estimators.

Conclusions and relevance to statistical inference

Recorded results

In the recorded experiment, the final sample size was n = 1000 observations per attacker. Scores followed the discrete uniform distribution on {0, 1, …, 10}, with theoretical mean μ = 5 and variance σ² = 10.

The average sample mean was 4.9990, close to 5. The average corrected sample variance was 10.0065, close to 10. The average descriptive variance was 9.9965, close to its theoretical expectation of 9.99.

These values describe one recorded run. New simulations produce different empirical results.

Unbiasedness does not imply precision

The sample mean and corrected sample variance are unbiased: E[X̄ₙ] = μ and E[sₙ²] = σ². This means that their expectations match the population parameters. It does not mean that every individual sample produces the correct values.

At a sample size of 5, the average corrected sample variance was already 10.0337, close to the target 10. However, the variance of those estimates across attackers was 26.1812, showing substantial variability.

At a sample size of 1000, the average corrected sample variance was 10.0065, while its between-attacker variance had fallen to 0.0781. The estimator remained centered near the same target but became much more precise.

Increasing sample size reduces sampling variability

The variance of the sample mean is σ²/n = 10/n. In the experiment, the empirical variance of sample means decreased from 1.9317 at n = 5 to 0.0098 at n = 1000. The corresponding theoretical values are 2 and 0.01.

The sample mean therefore becomes more concentrated around 5 as the number of systems attempted by each attacker increases. For the chosen bounded distribution, the corrected sample variance also becomes concentrated around 10. Both estimators are consistent.

Consistency concerns convergence as sample size increases. It differs from unbiasedness, which concerns the expectation of an estimator at a fixed sample size. Finite-sample estimates may fluctuate between checkpoints.

Why divide by n − 1?

Deviations are measured from the sample mean, which was itself estimated from the same observations. These deviations sum to zero, leaving n − 1 degrees of freedom. Under independent, identically distributed sampling, E[SSₙ] = (n − 1)σ².

Dividing by n − 1 therefore gives an unbiased variance estimator. Dividing by n gives expectation [(n − 1)/n]σ², which is smaller than σ² when σ² is positive. For our distribution, this expectation is 8 at n = 5 and 9.99 at n = 1000.

The downward bias becomes smaller as n increases. Although SSₙ/n is biased at finite sample sizes, it is also consistent for the population variance. Bias does not automatically imply inconsistency.

Why this matters for inferential statistics

In the simulation, the population parameters are known, so we can assess the estimators. In an actual statistical investigation, these parameters are usually unknown and must be estimated from observed data.

Each attacker represents one possible sample. Comparing many attackers approximates the sampling distribution of an estimator: the distribution of estimates obtained by repeating the sampling procedure. This reveals how much an estimate may vary from sample to sample.

The standard deviation of the sample mean is its standard error: σ/√n. When σ is unknown, it can be estimated using s/√n. Standard errors help quantify uncertainty and construct confidence intervals under appropriate assumptions.

For example, quadrupling the sample size halves the theoretical standard error of the sample mean. More observations improve precision, but the improvement follows a square-root relationship rather than a linear one.

The different roles of n and m

Increasing n gives each attacker more observations and reduces the variability of that attacker's estimators. Increasing m gives the experiment more repeated samples, improving our estimates of the sampling distributions. It does not change the theoretical sampling distribution at a fixed n.

The average of the corrected sample variances describes within-attacker score variability and estimates σ² = 10. The variance of the sample means describes between-sample variability and estimates σ²/n. These are different quantities.

What Welford contributes

Welford's recurrence computes the same mean and sum of squared deviations as the corresponding batch calculation, up to floating-point rounding. It updates the statistics sequentially with constant memory for its running state.

This is useful for streams of security events or measurements, where observations arrive over time and storing the entire history may be unnecessary or impractical. Welford changes how the statistics are computed; it does not change their statistical properties.

Limitations

The experiment assumes independent observations from a fixed distribution and independence between attackers. Real penetration scores may exhibit dependence, changing probabilities or differences between systems and attackers. Such features would require a different statistical model.

Checkpoints reuse the same trajectories, so their results are dependent. Histograms also depend on the number of attackers, interval widths and axis settings. The simulation illustrates the theoretical properties; it does not replace their mathematical justification.
